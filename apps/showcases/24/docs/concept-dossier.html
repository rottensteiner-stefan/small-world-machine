The Shader Gallery

Showcase 24 — a “planetarium of shaders”: The observer stands inside a sphere and looks at monitors fitted to the curvature of the sphere’s inner surface.

WebGL2 · Shadertoy / GLSLSandbox (GLSL) WebGPU · ComputeToys (WGSL) optical trick · observer at the inner wall

The Concept

The original idea of Showcase 24: An observer stands inside a sphere. All around, on the inside of the sphere’s surface, hang several “monitors”, each running a different shader — an immersive tour through the engine’s shader collection, presented like a domed planetarium gallery.

Each monitor is geometrically fitted to the sphere’s inner surface: Its plane geometry is bent along the sphere by ScreenCurvatureBehavior, so at rest it lies exactly on the line of the wireframe reference sphere — not as a flat image, but as part of the surface.

VR-like Environment

24 monitors in three rings (+35°/0°/−35°), spread over 360°. You stand in the gallery and turn through a full 360° with mouselook.

Focus Mode

Clicking a monitor zooms in and flattens it for reading; clicking empty space or Esc returns to the start position and curves it back onto the sphere.

Backend Showdown

WebGL2 shows GLSL shaders (Shadertoy/GLSLSandbox), WebGPU the WGSL counterparts (ComputeToys). Layout and geometry are identical — only the shader source differs.

The Optical Trick

Why the curvature becomes visible: A monitor 3 units wide on a sphere of radius 10 sags only ~0.2 units at the edges — invisible from 10+ units away. The solution is geometric: The sphere is made smaller (radius 5.5) and the observer stands not in the centre, but with their back close to the inner wall. This way the surrounding monitors are seen from very close and strongly foreshortened in perspective — their curvature unmistakably follows the lines of the wireframe model.

This is the core detail of the staging (OBSERVER_BASE in showcase.ts): The viewer’s position decides whether the geometry “fitted” to the sphere works as an illusion or not. An eccentric point inside the sphere is the only honest way to get there — the curvature itself is not the spectacle, the perspective on it is.

Interaction

  • Mouse — 360° mouselook (the observer stays fixed at the inner wall)
  • Click on monitor — fly in, flatten the monitor (reading mode)
  • Click on empty space / Esc — back to the inner wall, the monitor curves again

Implementation Notes / Architecture

  • GALLERY_RADIUS = 5.5 — gallery sphere; every monitor is bent onto exactly this sphere.
  • WIREFRAME_RADIUS = GALLERY_RADIUS + 0.03 — the wireframe reference sphere sits a hair outside the monitor surfaces so edges and grid lines do not overdraw each other (z-fighting avoidance).
  • True grid curvature instead of simple bending: Every monitor is a real sphere patch. Its left/right edge runs along a line of longitude (meridian, constant θ), its top/bottom edge along a line of latitude (parallel, constant φ) — exactly on the line of the wireframe model (ScreenCurvatureBehavior). At curvature 1 all vertices lie on the sphere, at 0 in the flat tangent plane (reading mode on click).
  • Orientation via an explicit basis (Matrix4→Quaternion): local +X = latitude, +Y = longitude tangent, +Z = toward the sphere centre. Verified empirically with an engine probe.
  • Quality hook window.__showcase24.report() returns, per monitor, the orientation dot products, a bend-probe vertex and the observer distances — for reproducible audits.
  • Interaction via raycaster on the (collidable) monitor objects; focus and zoom logic unchanged from the earlier version.

Aesthetics & Tone

Dark, technical look: the blue wireframe sphere as the gallery’s “architecture”, the glowing animated shader monitors as the scene’s only light sources. Deliberately sparse lighting (directional + ambient only for surface detail) — the focus is entirely on the shaders.
